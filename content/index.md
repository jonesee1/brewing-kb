## Beer Brewing Knowledge Base

This site contains information about the basic process of brewing beer, information about some popular styles and a few recipes.  The site is open to the public and additions are welcome.

## Brewing Process Overview
This site is focused on home brewing, but the process is basically the same in the home and in the brewery.  It's about converting malt starch to sugar, boiling the wort (the liquid) with hops to add the intended flavor and then converting that sugar to some amount of alcohol using brewing yeast.  For home brewers there are three main ways to brew.

#### Extract
Extract brewing is the easiest way to make a beer, and is typically the way a beginner gets started.  Home brew stores have kits that contain a syrup like liquid or a powder which has already completed the process of converting the malt starch to sugar.  This means you simply boil the the extract in at leas 3.5 gallons of water and add the hops based on the recipe.  You then add enough water to get to 5 gallons in a 5 gallon bucket used to ferment (converting the sugar to alcohol).  Once fermentation is complete, you need to carbonate.  Some carbonation is created during fermentation, but not enough for the finished product.  If you are bottling, you can add carbonating sugar so the remaining yeast will convert it to CO2.  More experienced home brewers may keg their batches, typically in used 5 gallon soda kegs and use a CO2 tank to carbonate and add pressure to serve the batch.

#### Partial Grain
Partial grain brewing is similar to the extract process, but you add some grain to the boil.  This can be used to add flavor or color or both.  There are mesh bags sold at home brew stores and the processes is similar to steeping tea.  You dip the grain in an few times, let it sit and dip again until you get the desired results.  Often the recipe will have some advice for the process.  During this time you are adding hops based on the recipe you were given or created and the process after is the same as full extract.  You either bottle or keg and carbonate.  

#### All Grain
All grain brewing is the brewery process.  It involves taking grains, often a mix of different types, and mashing those to create sugar from the starch in the grains.  In the mash process you put the grain in what's called a mash tun and add water heated to a specified temperature which is usually between 160f - 180f.  Give it a few stirs and let it sit for around an hour.  Each recipe is different so times may vary.  It's important to monitor the temperature during the mash and if it drops too much, add some hot water to recover.  After the mash, you drain the wort (the liquid) into a boil kettle and the process from there is the same as the other styles.  Boil adding hops, then ferment using the chosen yeast.

## Key Terms
### Mash
A mash or mashing is the process of converting the starch in grain to sugar.  The grain is typically crushed slightly creating a flour and making the starch more accessible.  Most homebrewers use a modified cooler as a mash tun to perform the process.  The modifications are a drain valve with some kind of filter to prevent the grain from getting into the wort.  It is very important to monitor the temperature of the mash as the amount of heat influences the conversion of starches to sugar.

### Boil
The boil is where most beers get the flavor.  It's typically about an hour with hops added early for bitterness and later for flavor.  It's a straightforward process, but is an essential part of any brewing process.  

### Hops
Hops are a flower that when heated release an oil that can provide bitterness or citrus and floral flavors.  Hops are grown the U.S., England, Germany and a few other countries.  Each region produces specific characteristics so it's a good idea to experiment with different varieties if you can.

### Kettle
The boil kettle is simply a large pot capable of boiling 3.5 to 5 gallons of water.  The size needs to account for the expansion of the water under heat so 6 to 7 gallon pots are common.  Many are also fitted with a valve to drain the finished product once cooled.

### Cooling
The cooling process prepares the finished wort for fermentation after the boil.  The simplest, but least effective way is to move the boil kettle to an ice bath.  This displaces the heat, but takes a long time.  Another common method is to use a cooling tube.  This is a copper or stainless tube coiled to be able to fit inside the kettle with tubing attached to each end.  One end takes cold water into the tube and the other lets hot water drain out.

### Fermenter
The fermenter in a homebrew system is typically a 5 gallon plastic bucket with a lid.  You add the cooled wort to the bucket and the yeast then stir the mixture a bit.  A hole is drilled into the lid to add an air bubbler,  This device is attached to the lid using a cork and when the yeast starts to processes the sugars, you will see bubbles in the water.  Once the bubbles stop after a few says, the yeast is likely done and it's time to bottle or keg.  You can either siphon the beer out or install a valve a few inches up from the bottom of the bucket.  The positioning of the valve helps reduce the chance you get sludge from the yeast in the finished product.

### Density/Gravity
The density or gravity of wort, the boiled product, and the gravity of the fermented product is used to determine the alcohol content.  This can be measured in both stages using a hydrometer which floats in the liquid and has a gauge that measures the density of the fluid.  You can also use a refractometer which is mostly used in wine making to measure the sugar content. Everything is compared to water, which has a specific gravity of 1.00.  From a brewing perspective, anything above 1.00 indicates some sugar is present.  Taking a reading before fermentation and one after will give you the alcohol content of the batch.  For example if you have a pre fermentation reading of 1.046 (Original Gravity) and a post fermentation reading of 1.010 (Final Gravity), you subtract the FG from the OG and multiply the result by 131.25 your end up with an estimated 4.7% alcohol by volume (ABV).
